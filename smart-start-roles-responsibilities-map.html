The Roles and Responsibilities Map

This will produce a clear picture of what the business needs, and who is actually holding it.

15 to 20 minutes
Nothing is saved, print what you need
Before you start: ownership rarely gets assigned on purpose as a business grows, it accumulates by accident, one urgent moment at a time. This is a three-part exercise to step back and see the whole shape of the business at once, so the gaps and the overloaded people both become visible together, rather than being noticed one crisis at a time. Grab a pen and paper or a whiteboard if you have one, the mapping stage works best when you can freely draw and adjust it.
1

Map everything the business does

Whatever a business actually does, its work tends to fall into four main areas. Start with these four rather than trying to work your own out from scratch:

  • Sales and marketing, finding and winning the work
  • Operations, delivering it
  • Finance and admin, running the numbers and the paperwork
  • People, everyone who works in the business, and how they're supported

Under each of the four, list the actual, specific tasks that happen day to day, for example "raise an invoice" under finance and admin, or "schedule posts on Instagram" under sales and marketing. You're listing what needs to happen here, not how to do it, the how-to for any of these can be its own document later if it's useful.

Don't aim for a perfect or complete list on the first pass. This is a brain dump, the goal is to get everything out of your head and onto the page. You can always come back and add to it.

Teal = one of the four main areas, coral = a specific task within it. Do this for all four, this shows just two as a worked example.

List the specific tasks under each of the four areas here, or on paper.
2

Group related tasks into roles

Now look back across everything you've mapped, and start grouping tasks that would make sense for one person to own together. Don't group them the way a large company would, by department, a small business rarely has the headcount for that. Group by what would genuinely work as a single, coherent job in a business your size.

A few things worth looking for as you group:

  • Similar skills. Tasks that draw on the same kind of ability often belong together, writing social posts and writing email newsletters are both a form of the same skill.
  • The same point in a process. Tasks that happen at the same stage often suit the same person, whoever takes the first client call might also make sense to run the onboarding that follows it.
  • Don't force a tidy structure. In a small team it's completely normal for one role to include things that would sit in three separate departments at a bigger company. That's not messy, that's just what small businesses look like.

You don't need an equal split or a fixed number of roles. Some groupings will end up with five or more tasks in them, others with just one, and that's fine too.

3

Build two simple charts

The role chart

Take the groupings you circled in step 2, each one becomes a role here. List each one with what it's responsible for and what it reports into. This chart shows all the work that needs to happen, as a set of roles, independent of who is actually doing the role right now. That makes it useful even for a role that doesn't have a dedicated person yet, or one that's currently being covered part time by someone doing several jobs at once.

Spot the overload

Look at the "roles they hold" column on your human chart, the roles you defined in step 3. Anyone holding three or more is worth a closer look, that's usually where things are quietly slipping through the cracks, or exactly where the next hire should go.

For each overloaded person, ask: could one of their roles genuinely move to someone else as it stands today? Could it be simplified or automated instead? Or does it point to a real, new role that's worth defining properly?

i
Before you act on any of this

If a change coming out of this map could affect someone's contract, hours, pay or job title, get proper HR or legal advice before you make it. What counts as a straightforward internal reshuffle and what counts as a formal restructure isn't always obvious, and getting that wrong creates a real legal problem, not just an awkward conversation.

Bring your team into this process itself, not just the outcome. If people sense you're quietly reviewing who does what without knowing why, it reads as surveillance. Be upfront that you're looking at workload and capacity across the business, not evaluating any one person, most people are relieved to hear that, not defensive.

Before you restructure anything

Don't try to fix everything you find here in one sitting. Pick the single most overloaded role, or the clearest gap, and start there. This map will still be true next month if you want to come back and work through the rest of it.

Your next step

If the overload points toward a genuinely new role, "What does this role need to achieve?" will help you define it properly before writing a job description. If it points to work that could be redesigned, automated or outsourced instead, "What's the smartest way to get the work done?" covers those options.
