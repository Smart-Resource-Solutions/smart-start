The Process Audit

Work out whether this is a knowledge problem or a process problem, then fix the right one.

About 5 minutes

Why this is worth ten minutes: a process problem left alone doesn't just cost time, it costs money and goodwill too, through rework, missed deadlines, and people working around a broken system instead of through it.

There are two common reasons work gets stuck like this. Either one specific task only works because you, or one person, knows how to do it, or a process that already involves several people has just become clunky and error prone. Jump to whichever sounds closer to what's actually happening below.

Capture and hand it off

For one task that only works because you, or one person, know how to do it

Why writing it down doesn't work

Most business owners have tried to write a process document at least once. It usually goes the same way: it takes three hours, it's out of date within a month, and nobody reads it anyway, because reading five pages to learn a task is slower than just asking you.

A screen recording fixes all three problems at once. It takes the same amount of time as just doing the task. It shows the real version, mouse clicks, hesitations and all, not the tidied up version you'd write from memory. And people will actually watch a short video before they'll read a manual.

If the task is genuinely small or simple, a couple of clear screenshots with a short note on each may be enough, you don't need a full video walkthrough for something that only takes thirty seconds to explain. Save the recording for anything with several steps, or anything where the order matters.

Ask yourselfIf you were unreachable for two weeks, would this task simply stop, or would someone else find a way to muddle through it? If it would genuinely stop, this is worth capturing now.

The 3-step handoff

  1. Record it once, out loud. Open a screen recorder (Windows' Snipping Tool now records video too, worth checking that's the one you meant) and make sure your sound is turned on before you start. Do the task exactly as you normally would, and narrate it as if you're training someone standing behind you. Say the why, not just the what.
  2. Send the raw file, unedited. Don't trim it, don't add titles, don't wait until you have time to make it good. A slightly messy video sent today beats a polished one sent never.
  3. Attach one Definition of Done. A video shows someone how. It doesn't tell them when they've finished correctly. Pair every recording with the template below.

Definition of Done: fill in the blank

This task,  , is complete when:
  •   has been sent to  
  •   is true or has been checked
  •   has been updated with the result

It is not complete if  .

If something looks wrong or unusual, stop and message   rather than guessing.

Before you record

Don't wait for the important processes to justify doing this. Start with whichever task you were most recently annoyed to be doing yourself, that's usually the one costing you the most, and the fastest one to hand off.

Your next step

Once it's recorded, sent, and paired with its Definition of Done, use the Delegation Brief to make sure the handoff actually sticks, who owns it now, what they can decide without checking with you, and when you'll check in. If it turns out this same task keeps landing back on your desk even after handing it off, that's a sign the audit or the decision behind it, not the task, is still yours.

Map and simplify it

For a process that already involves several people, and is just clunky or error prone

Why this usually isn't a people problem

When a process feels slow, confusing or error prone, it's tempting to assume someone isn't doing their job properly. Often the truth is simpler: the process itself has too many handoffs, too much re-entering of the same information, or steps that made sense once and never got removed.

Hiring more people into the same clunky process just makes the process bigger, not better. Worth mapping it properly before deciding anything else.

Map it

Pick the one process causing the most frustration or wasted time. The first two questions just help you see the whole thing clearly, the rest are where the real answers show up.

What starts the process? 
What happens next? 
Who touches it, and how many people? 
Where does it sit and wait? 
Where is the same information entered more than once? 
Where do mistakes or rework actually happen? 
Which steps could disappear entirely? 

What your answers are telling you

  • Three or more people touch it: that's often too many handoffs, look at who genuinely needs to be involved.
  • The same information gets entered more than once: a strong candidate for automation, not more capacity.
  • It sits and waits somewhere: that's usually a queue or an approval step worth questioning, not a workload problem.
  • Mistakes cluster at one particular step: that step is the one to fix first, not the person currently doing it.
  • You can already name steps that could disappear entirely: that's usually the fastest, cheapest fix available, remove those before you touch anything else.

Before you conclude anything

Don't assume the person running the process is the problem until you've mapped it. Most process pain turns out to be structural, and blaming the person doing it just moves the same broken process to someone new.

Your next step

If simplifying or automating this looks like the answer, "What's the smartest way to get the work done?" can help you work through the options properly.
