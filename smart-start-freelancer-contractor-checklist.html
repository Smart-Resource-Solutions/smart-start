Freelancer and Contractor Checklist

Borrowing capability in works best when a few basics are agreed upfront, not discovered halfway through.

Read this before anything else

Engaging a freelancer or contractor incorrectly can have genuine legal and tax consequences, in the UK this includes employment status and IR35 rules, and getting it wrong can mean the engagement is later treated as employment, with backdated tax, National Insurance and employment rights implications.

This checklist is a practical starting point, not legal or tax advice. Before engaging anyone, get proper advice on employment status and IR35 from an accountant, employment solicitor, or HMRC's own guidance, particularly if the engagement is likely to be ongoing or full time in all but name.

Before you engage them

Get these agreed before any work starts, not after.

The brief is specific. What outcome you need, not just a number of hours or days.
Genuine independence. They control how and when the work gets done, use their own equipment where practical, and could reasonably send a substitute. If none of that is true, this may look like employment, not a genuine contractor arrangement.
Rate and payment terms are agreed in writing. Including when and how invoices will be paid.
They have their own insurance where relevant to the work, professional indemnity or public liability.
IP and ownership of the work is clear. Who owns what they produce once it's paid for.

Get it in writing

A written agreement protects both sides, not just the business.

A written contract or agreement exists, not just an email thread or a verbal understanding.
Scope, deliverables and deadline are named specifically inside the agreement, not left implied.
Confidentiality and data handling are covered if they'll have access to client information or business systems.
Notice or termination terms are stated, so either side knows how to end the arrangement cleanly.

During the engagement

Treating them consistently as a contractor matters as much as the paperwork.

Access is limited to what the work actually needs, not the same systems access as an employee.
They aren't managed like an employee, fixed hours, mandatory meetings, or day to day supervision all weaken the case that this is genuine contractor work.
Invoices are paid on the agreed terms, not treated informally like payroll.

Before you treat this as settled

Do not assume a signed contract alone protects you if the actual working relationship looks like employment in practice. Status is judged on how the arrangement genuinely works day to day, not just on what the paperwork says.

Where to go from here

If this engagement is likely to become ongoing or substantial, it may be worth revisiting whether it should become a defined role instead. "What does this role need to achieve?" can help you think that through properly.

This is not a definitive or exhaustive list, and the legal points above reflect UK employment status and IR35 rules specifically. If you're operating outside the UK, check the equivalent employment and tax rules that apply in your own country before engaging anyone.
